# CEOs Are Starting to See Value from AI. Now Comes Execution.
**Authors:** Tuukka Seppä, Matthieu Berthion, Dominic C. Klemmer, Tomas Nordahl, Nicolas de Bellefonds, Kristine Buus

**Category:** Strategy & Governance

**Published:** 22 July 2026

## Key Points
- Nearly nine in ten CEOs report cost or revenue benefits from AI in targeted areas, but only 26% have embedded AI within a broader business transformation while 64% are still running pilots.
- The widest gap between recognition and action is financial: 56% of CEOs name the unclear link between AI initiatives and specific financial outcomes as a barrier, yet only 14% define P&L impact for every initiative.
- BCG attributes roughly 10% of AI value to algorithms, 20% to data, and the remaining 70% to changes in the operating model and ways of working.
- High performers, defined as achieving at least 10% cost reduction or 5% revenue growth from AI, are about seven times more likely to redesign workflows end to end and five times more likely to define a P&L impact for each initiative.
- The recommended posture is the CEO as orchestrator setting direction, funding, and risk appetite while CXOs and P&L owners hold execution accountability, rather than outsourcing AI strategy to a chief AI officer.

## Summary
Drawing on the BCG AI Transformation CEO Survey 2026, which covers 152 chief executives at companies with revenues of at least US$500 million, this article argues that enterprise AI has moved past the question of whether it works. Nearly nine in ten CEOs now report cost or revenue benefits in targeted areas. The unresolved problem is scale: 64% of respondents are pursuing pilots while only 26% have embedded AI inside a broader business transformation. BCG frames the shortfall as an execution gap rather than a technology gap, and the survey data shows CEOs consistently naming barriers they have not yet funded or structurally addressed. The largest of these gaps is a 42-point spread between the 56% who cite unclear links between AI work and financial outcomes and the 14% who actually define P&L impact for all initiatives. Comparable gaps appear on redesigning people and workflows (55% cite it as a barrier, around 32% fully fund it) and on accountability (55% see the need, 30% have restructured for it).

The article sets out four moves. First, make the CEO the orchestrator but the business accountable: the CEO sets strategic direction, secures board authorisation of ambition, funding, and enterprise risk appetite, and then cascades accountability to CXOs and individual project owners with metrics tied to outcomes rather than activity. Fewer than 10% of CEOs believe AI strategy should be handed to a chief AI officer, and nearly half say they personally lead implementation. Second, focus AI on a small number of high-value domains that can change the business, using cross-functional teams that combine business, technology, and change management capability, rather than experimenting everywhere. Third, prioritise people and change management, which is where BCG locates 70% of the value; high performers are 2.4 times more likely to assign their best talent to AI workstreams and roughly twice as likely to adapt incentives, alter decision rights, and fully fund the leadership and change components. Fourth, set projects up so value can be tracked: define expected P&L impact, baseline, KPIs, and named owners before launch, treat the business case as a living document reviewed on a regular cycle such as every twelve weeks, and use a transformation office and a single source of truth to manage dependencies. High performers are 1.4 times more likely to report that finance enforces P&L accountability.

BCG also explains why AI transformations behave differently from previous ones. The people impact is greater because AI replaces skills and reshapes how teams collaborate rather than simply changing tasks. The technology keeps evolving mid-implementation, so plans need frequent course correction. Risks scale faster than understanding, with new failure modes spanning cyber vulnerabilities, unpredictable running costs, and vendor lock-in. And value is harder to prove, arriving later and attributing less cleanly than in conventional transformation programmes. The comparison between high and lower performers reinforces the point that the differentiators are managerial rather than technical: clear accountability (46% versus 24%), reshaping the business with AI (14% versus 2%), tracking financial value to the P&L (25% versus 5%), and investing in people and change (44% versus 24%). The practical implication for boards and executive teams is that the next phase of AI value depends on applying ordinary transformation discipline, not on further experimentation.
